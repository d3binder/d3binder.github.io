Working with a dealership

General guidance, not a script — every dealership and every deal is different, but these habits tend to hold up.

01

Before you go

  • Know the fair price first. Check a couple of independent sources (KBB, Edmunds, TrueCar, manufacturer invoice pricing) so you recognize a fair number when you see one.
  • Get pre-approved financing from your own bank or credit union before you go. Even if you end up financing through the dealer, having your own rate in hand gives you something real to compare against.
  • Set your out-the-door budget in advance — one number that already includes tax, title, and fees, not just "the payment."
02

At the dealership

  • Negotiate the out-the-door price, not the monthly payment. A lower payment can just mean a longer loan or a worse rate — the total price is what actually matters.
  • Keep price, trade-in, and financing as three separate conversations. Bundling them together makes it easy to lose track of whether any one of them is actually a good deal.
  • You're allowed to leave. A "today only" price, a long wait meant to wear you down, or pressure to decide on the spot are common tactics — a good deal is still good tomorrow.
03

Financing & the paperwork

  • A longer loan term lowers the payment but usually costs more overall in interest, and it takes longer before you owe less than the car is worth.
  • Watch for "yo-yo financing" — driving off before financing is fully finalized, then getting a call days later asking you to sign a worse deal. If the financing isn't final, the deal isn't final.
  • Read every line before you sign. Extended warranties, gap insurance, paint protection, and other add-ons are often pre-checked or bundled in, and can usually be declined or negotiated on their own.
04

Total cost of ownership

  • The sticker price is just the start. Insurance, fuel, and maintenance over a few years often add up to more than the loan itself — that's what the "True Monthly Cost" above tries to capture.
  • Get an insurance quote before you buy, not after. Premiums vary a lot by make and model, and a great deal on the car can be erased by a higher-than-expected insurance bill.
  • For a used car, an independent pre-purchase inspection — from a mechanic you chose, not the seller's — is one of the cheapest ways to avoid a costly surprise.
