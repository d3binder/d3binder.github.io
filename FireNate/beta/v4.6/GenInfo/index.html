The IRS typically announces the following year's 401(k) limits in late October or early November — the figures shown for 2027 are actuarial forecasts (based on inflation trends) and will be confirmed once the IRS releases its official notice, likely around November 2026.
What's the "Super Catch-Up"? Starting in 2025, SECURE 2.0 introduced a higher catch-up contribution limit for savers who turn 60, 61, 62, or 63 during the year — it replaces (rather than stacks on top of) the standard 50+ catch-up for that age band. It didn't exist prior to 2025, which is why those cells are blank for 2022–2024.

Your traditional and Roth IRA contributions share one combined limit — $7,500 total across both (or $8,600 if you're 50+), not $7,500 each. SEP IRAs are funded by the employer (or by you, if self-employed) rather than through employee payroll deferrals, so there's no employee catch-up. SIMPLE IRA catch-up amounts vary depending on whether your employer has adopted the higher small-employer limits available since SECURE 2.0.
Roth & traditional IRA income limits (2026): Roth IRA eligibility phases out between $153,000–$168,000 MAGI (single) or $242,000–$252,000 (married filing jointly). If you're covered by a workplace plan, your traditional IRA deduction phases out between $81,000–$91,000 (single) or $129,000–$149,000 (married, contributor covered); if only your spouse is covered, the range is $242,000–$252,000. Above the top of these ranges, a "backdoor Roth" conversion is a common workaround.

Born 1943–1954: 66  •  1955: 66 & 2 mo  •  1956: 66 & 4 mo  •  1957: 66 & 6 mo  •  1958: 66 & 8 mo  •  1959: 66 & 10 mo  •  1960 or later: 67
Claiming before your FRA (as early as 62) permanently reduces your monthly benefit; delaying past FRA (up to age 70) permanently increases it. These figures assume a hypothetical maximum earner — most people's actual benefit will be lower and depends entirely on your own earnings history, which you can check anytime at ssa.gov/myaccount.
